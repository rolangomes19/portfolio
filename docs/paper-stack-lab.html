01 The lighting in your references

Yes it transfers — but it is not a gradient, and it does not belong on the paper

I measured the luminance field in all three posters. It is not one radial highlight. There are three overlapping soft lobes plus one hard dark corner, swinging about ±23% around the mean, blurred past the point where you can find an edge. That irregularity is the entire reason it reads as a photograph of a lit surface instead of CSS.

Correction to what I gave you last week. My R5 recipe was two clean radial gradients. Geometrically tidy, and it will look computer-generated next to these. Replace it with the field below.
/* the mat's light field — irregular on purpose */
.mat::before{
  position: absolute; inset: -14%;
  filter: blur(72px);
  mix-blend-mode: soft-light;
  background:
    radial-gradient(38% 30% at 26% 22%, rgb(255 255 255/.92), transparent 68%),
    radial-gradient(46% 36% at 63% 58%, rgb(255 255 255/.80), transparent 70%),
    radial-gradient(30% 22% at 14% 78%, rgb(255 255 255/.44), transparent 72%),
    radial-gradient(44% 34% at 92%  6%, rgb(0 0 0/.95),        transparent 66%),
    radial-gradient(40% 30% at  4%104%, rgb(0 0 0/.70),        transparent 70%);
}
/* ponytail: gradients + blur is fine at this scale. If paint cost
   shows on scroll, render it once to a 400px JPG and scale to cover —
   a blurred image loses nothing when you stretch it. */
Lobes
Three light, two dark. Never symmetrical, never centred. One dark lobe must sit hard in a corner — that is what stops it reading as a vignette.
Blur
60–80px. Below 40px you can see the gradient shapes and the illusion dies.
Blend
soft-light, not screen or overlay. soft-light keeps the mat's hue and only moves luminance, so the green stays green in both the lit and shaded areas.
Where
Mat only. This is the biggest craft note on the page. The paper must catch a flatter, duller light than the surface under it — that difference in specularity is how an eye separates two materials. Put the same bloom on both and the paper welds itself to the mat.
Grain
Your references are grainy — far heavier than the 3–5% I specified. Mat 10–12% over overlay. Paper 4% over multiply. One noise tile, two opacities. Heavy grain on cream paper just looks dirty.
Weave
Your mat mockup has two grid frequencies — a 46px printed grid in white, and a 4px cross-hatch underneath at 3–5%. The fine one is the self-healing surface. Without it you have graph paper, not a cutting mat.

03 The idea in your reference images

Your three posters are not a colour study. They are your dark mode.

Light mode is graphite on cream paper lying on a green cutting mat. Dark mode is the same drawing as a cyanotype on a backlit light table — white line work, glowing slightly, on saturated blue. Identical geometry, identical grid, identical type. Only the material changes.

That is a concept-true dark mode rather than a colour inversion, it explains itself in one sentence in an interview, and it is the reason the emissive line glow in those posters is correct there and would be wrong on your printed mat. Lines on paper absorb light. Lines on a light table emit it.

:root[data-theme="dark"]{
  --paper:     #123A8F;   /* cyanotype ground   */
  --mat:       #081F55;   /* light table, off   */
  --grid:      rgb(255 255 255 / .30);
  --ink:       #EAF0FF;
  --grain-op:  .14;       /* up from .11        */
}
:root[data-theme="dark"] h1{
  text-shadow: 0 0 14px rgb(190 214 255 / .45),
               0 0 3px  rgb(255 255 255 / .35);
}

One check. The bloom costs contrast. text-shadow on body copy at this strength will fail AA. Glow the display type and the rules only; keep paragraphs crisp.

05 Contrast gate WCAG 2.1 AA · measured, not estimated

The grid was failing AA, so realism lost the argument

Text on graph paper is not measured against the paper. It is measured against the darkest thing it crosses, which is the grid line. Rev A got that wrong. Every ratio below is computed, and the fix was lightening two line colours and darkening one ink — the grid is still legible as a grid at 1.12:1 and 1.25:1 against the paper.

06 Edges two masks · 8KB + 15KB · tile at any width

A sheet on a cutting mat is blade-cut, so the main sheet should not be torn

That is the honest answer to your question. The mat exists because you cut straight lines on it — a torn hero sheet contradicts the whole set. So the clean cut edge stays the default, and the torn edge is reserved for the two places it is actually true: a page ripped out of a notebook inside a case study, and the kraft folder tab. Below, all three so you can see the difference side by side.

CUT — the default. 1px lit line on the cut face. Zero assets.
TORN — notebook page. Fibre notches, deep nicks.
DECKLE — handmade stock. Soft undulation, no spikes.
How it works
Two layered mask-images: the PNG strip repeating along the top edge, plus a solid gradient covering the rest. One 2400×36px alpha strip repeats seamlessly at any sheet width — no per-breakpoint assets.
The gotcha
A mask erases box-shadow. Move the shadow to a wrapper as filter: drop-shadow() — which is better anyway, because drop-shadow follows the torn silhouette instead of a rectangle. This is the detail that would have cost you an afternoon.
Generated, not drawn
Summed sine bands at integer frequencies (so it tiles) plus 46 random fibre notches, feathered 0.6px. A Python script, run once, committed. Never regenerate and never send an image through a model.

07 Vellum + NDA redaction your idea, built

The overlay carries the marker, and the client name is not in the page at all

Non-negotiable. A black bar drawn over real text is not redaction, it is a disclosure bug — Ctrl+A, view-source, or a screen reader all reveal it. The sensitive string must be absent from the DOM. The bar is a stand-in for content that was never sent, with an aria-label so assistive tech reports the withholding rather than silence. Shipping the fake version on a portfolio that sells NDA discipline would be the worst possible bug on your site.

Markup
<span class="redact" style="--ch:22" aria-label="Client name withheld under NDA"></span> — empty element, width driven by an approximate character count so the bar reads as the right length without encoding the actual string length precisely.
Why it looks real
Two overlapping marker passes at slightly different rotations, a 0.4px blur on the second, and an inset shadow for ink bleeding into the fibre. A single sharp rectangle reads as a CSS div; a real marker never covers in one stroke.
Why vellum
The redaction sits on the overlay, not the drawing. That is literally how a reviewed drawing works, and it means the same case study can be published at two clearance levels by swapping one sheet.

08 Arabic Reem Kufi + IBM Plex Sans Arabic · verified live on Google Fonts

Kufi is the architectural script, so the concept picks the display face for you

You asked for legibility at body size and beauty at title size. Those are two different faces, and one of them has a concept-true answer: Reem Kufi is a geometric Kufi, the script used in architecture, tilework and inscription for a thousand years. On a drafting table it is not a decorative choice, it is the right one. For body, IBM Plex Sans Arabic — a UI face with four usable weights, drawn against Plex's Latin metrics, so mixed lines do not fight.

Your untranslated-terms question, answered in one line

Put the Latin face first in the Arabic stack. Commissioner and Fraunces ship no Arabic subset — I checked the Google Fonts API, they carry latin, latin-ext, cyrillic, greek and vietnamese only. So the browser resolves font per character: Arabic characters find no glyph in Commissioner and fall through to Plex Arabic, while tokens, Code Connect and WCAG 2.1 AA hit Commissioner first and stay in the English face. This is the behaviour you asked for, and it needs no spans, no classes and no JavaScript.

/* Latin first, on the Arabic side too. This IS the mechanism. */
--f-text-ar:    "Commissioner", "IBM Plex Sans Arabic", system-ui, sans-serif;
--f-display-ar: "Fraunces",     "Reem Kufi",            Georgia,   serif;

[dir="rtl"], :lang(ar){
  letter-spacing: normal;          /* tracking shatters connected script */
  font-feature-settings: normal;
  line-height: 1.95;               /* ~+0.25 over Latin: dots and deep descenders */
}
The bug you will hit anyway. Font fallback is not bidi. An Arabic sentence containing Code Connect reorders wrongly at the boundaries, and adjacent punctuation jumps to the wrong end. Wrap every embedded Latin run in <bdi> — a native element whose entire purpose is this, defaulting to unicode-bidi: isolate. No CSS, no dir attribute, supported everywhere. The specimen above uses it.

Since it is configured
You said the Arabic pass already exists, so treat this as three swaps, not a pass: Harmattan → IBM Plex Sans Arabic (Harmattan is a SIL text face for West African Arabic, not a UI face — it thins out badly at 15–17px), El Messiri → Reem Kufi, and Latin-first stack ordering.
Labels in Arabic
Do not apply your uppercase +0.14em label treatment. Arabic has no case, and tracking breaks the joins. Signal a label with weight 600 and a size step instead — that is what .ar-label does above.
Numerals
Keep Western digits for technical figures. 80%, 35 → 0, WCAG 2.1 AA are read as terms of art by an enterprise Arabic reader; Eastern Arabic-Indic numerals would make your metrics harder to scan, not more localised.
Optical harmony knob
If Arabic looks slightly small beside the Latin, add size-adjust: 105% to a self-hosted Arabic @font-face rather than bumping font-size in a media query. Needs eyeballing, not a formula — I am not going to guess the value for you.
